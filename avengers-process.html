Avengers:
Doomsday.

Two versions, one lesson. A self-directed poster study that began with an overcrowded all-green composition and ended with a more controlled, cinematic hierarchy.

01 — The short story

From crowded scale to controlled clarity.

This page separates the actual visual assets from the explanation, so the viewer can understand the thinking behind the poster without feeling like they are reading a social post.

Step 01

Sketch the ambition.

The first direction tried to build an Avengers-level scale with a huge cast, dramatic energy fractures, and a tall pyramid composition.

Step 02

Find the failure.

The all-green palette created unity, but it also flattened depth. Too many characters started fighting for the same visual weight.

Step 03

Rebuild the threat.

The final version reduced the roster, sharpened Doom’s presence, and used green, purple, and warm light to create stronger rhythm.

02 — Process / Part I

The first layout had too much weight.

It started as a 27-character layout. The intention was big and cinematic, but the hierarchy was wrong and the frame became overcrowded.

The color plan also collapsed. The all-green palette buried the depth, and the character weight did not fully match the story tone.

So the design was scrapped, the structure was studied again, and the poster was rebuilt from zero instead of trying to force a weak direction to work.

Sometimes the first attempt fails only to show you the right direction.
03 — Version 01

The green version built the world.

Version 1 carries a strong, unified green identity. It established the original visual language of the project: cosmic, ominous, and Doom-centric.

The composition was ambitious, and the scale matched the tone of an Avengers-level threat. But the single-tone palette limited depth, and the character count pushed the layout close to saturation.

04 — Process / Part II

The rebuild made the frame breathe.

Instead of adding more, the final direction works by removing noise, balancing color temperature, and giving each major character a clearer role inside the composition.

This is the poster I saw in my head.

The final result reduced the cast to the core characters and fixed the character hierarchy from top to bottom.

It balanced green, purple, and cosmic yellow for a stronger Doomsday palette, then introduced energy fractures and multiverse elements without overwhelming the frame.

Dual Doom Threat

Doom appears with and without the mask, giving the poster a layered villain presence instead of a single background face.

Color Balance

Purple and warm highlights break the green dominance and guide the viewer through the main cast more naturally.

World-Building

The FF vehicle, lens work, cracks, and cosmic textures add scale while staying secondary to the main hierarchy.

05 — Version comparison

Scale versus clarity.

Move the slider to compare both poster directions. Version 1 explores scale. Version 2 refines the same idea into a cleaner cinematic system.

V2 clarity V1 scale

Version 1 — Scale

What worked: a strong green identity, an ominous cosmic mood, and a Doom-centric direction that felt large enough for an Avengers threat.

What struggled: the single-tone palette reduced depth, and the amount of characters pushed the design close to saturation.

Version 2 — Clarity

What improved: reducing the roster created stronger hierarchy, cleaner silhouettes, and clearer storytelling.

What refined it: the blend of green, purple, warm highlights, dual Doom masks, FF vehicle, lens work, and updated logo structure gave the poster a polished studio feel.

Both versions reflect different creative phases: one exploring scale, the other refining clarity. Together, they show progression, decision-making, and a better understanding of cinematic composition. There is always more to learn.

06 — One lesson

More characters do not always mean more cinema.

The biggest lesson from this poster was restraint. A strong cinematic frame needs clear hierarchy first. Scale matters, but the viewer should know where to look, what to feel, and why each element exists.

N° 01

Hierarchy first.

The viewer should read the main threat, heroes, and supporting elements in a controlled order.

N° 02

Color needs depth.

A single color can create identity, but contrast and temperature create depth and movement.

N° 03

Remove noise.

Reducing the cast made the final design feel more premium, not less epic.

N° 04

Build the world softly.

Details like vehicles, fractures, and textures should support the story, not compete with it.
